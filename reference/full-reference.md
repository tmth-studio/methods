# Part I — The Method

## 1. Why structure matters

Readers of business documents are busy, sceptical, and reading to decide, not to be entertained. When they encounter a stream of facts without a stated conclusion, they are forced to do the writer's job: hold each fact in working memory, guess at its relevance, and infer the point being built towards. Working memory is small — most people can hold only a handful of discrete items at once — so unstructured writing overloads the reader quickly, and the reader's inferred conclusion may not be the one the writer intended.

Worse, an unstructured document invites the reader to build their *own* structure as they go. Once they have guessed at your point, everything you write afterwards is read against that guess. If they guessed wrong, your actual conclusion arrives as a confusion or a contradiction rather than a landing.

The remedy is to do the sorting yourself, before writing, and to present the result top-down: conclusion first, then the reasoning that supports it, in layers of increasing detail. The reader always knows why they are reading each sentence, because the point it supports has already been stated.

This is harder than it sounds, because it inverts the order in which analysis happens. You discover your conclusion last; the reader should meet it first. The pyramid is the tool for making that inversion deliberate — and, just as importantly, for exposing the places where your analysis is not actually finished.

## 2. The core idea: answer first, support below

Every document should be organisable as a pyramid of ideas:

- **One governing thought at the top** — the single message the document exists to deliver.
- **A layer of supporting ideas beneath it** — typically three to five, each answering the question the governing thought provokes.
- **Further layers beneath each of those** — evidence, analysis, and detail, each layer answering the question raised by the idea above it.

Three rules define a valid pyramid:

1. **Ideas at any level summarise the ideas grouped below them.** A parent idea is not a label ("Findings") but a claim ("Churn is concentrated in customers onboarded through the partner channel").
2. **Ideas within a group are the same kind of thing.** All reasons, or all steps, or all problems — not a mix.
3. **Ideas within a group follow a deliberate order.** Time order, structural order, or ranking order — chosen, not accidental.

If you cannot state the parent idea of a group, the group is not yet thought through. If items in a group are different kinds of thing, the grouping is arbitrary. The pyramid's value is that it makes these failures visible before the reader finds them.

**Building the pyramid: two directions.** In practice you build from both ends and meet in the middle:

- *Top-down*, when you already know your answer: state the governing thought, ask what question it provokes, list the answers, and repeat downwards. This is fast but risks confirmation — you find only the support you went looking for.
- *Bottom-up*, when you have material but no message: list every point you believe you need to make, group the ones that belong together, name what each group adds up to, and keep summarising upwards until a single governing thought emerges. This is slower but honest — the conclusion is earned from the material rather than imposed on it.

Most real documents need a pass in each direction: bottom-up to discover the message, top-down to test that every branch genuinely supports it and to prune what doesn't. Anything that survives the grouping but supports no parent idea is cut, however hard-won the analysis behind it. The reader is buying the argument, not the effort.

## 3. Introductions: Situation → Complication → Question → Answer

The top of the pyramid does not float free; it must be introduced. The introduction's job is to remind the reader of what they already know and accept, so that the document's main message lands as the answer to a question the reader now genuinely holds. The classic sequence:

- **Situation.** A statement about the subject that the reader will recognise and agree with. It establishes shared ground. *"Northfield Logistics has grown depot capacity by 40% over three years to support its next-day delivery promise."*
- **Complication.** The development that disturbs the situation and creates tension. *"Utilisation of the new capacity is running at 55%, and depot overheads are now the largest single drag on operating margin."*
- **Question.** The question the complication raises in the reader's mind. Often left implicit. *"Should Northfield consolidate its depot network?"*
- **Answer.** The governing thought — your main message. *"Northfield should consolidate to a hub-and-spoke model around its four highest-throughput depots, which preserves the delivery promise while removing roughly a fifth of fixed cost."*

Why does this work? Because a message only feels relevant when it answers a question the reader is actually asking. The Situation–Complication pair *manufactures* that question in the reader's mind, from material they already accept, moments before you answer it. A document that opens with its answer cold asks the reader to care before giving them a reason; a document that opens with pages of background asks them to wait without knowing what for. SCQA does neither.

Disciplines that keep introductions honest:

- **The situation contains only what the reader already accepts.** New or contentious claims belong in the body, where they can be supported. If you find yourself defending a sentence in the introduction, it is in the wrong place.
- **The answer must actually answer the question the complication raises.** If it answers a different question, either the introduction or the document is misdirected. This mismatch is the single most common structural fault in draft documents, and the SCQ sequence exists to expose it.
- **Keep it short.** The introduction is a story the reader already knows, told to set up a question. It is not a literature review. For most documents, a paragraph or two.
- **Match the situation to the reader, not to you.** The shared ground is what *they* know. A document that opens with your team's activity ("Over the past six weeks we have conducted interviews...") starts from the writer's situation, not the reader's — a reliable sign the document is about to narrate the work rather than deliver the answer.

The four elements can be reordered for effect. Answer-first ("standard consulting opening") suits impatient senior readers; complication-first suits genuine urgency ("The regulator's deadline moved to March"); question-first suits terms-of-reference responses. All four elements should be present in some order — the reordering changes tone, not content.

## 4. Introduction patterns for common document types

The SCQA machinery adapts to recurring document types. The variable is usually the Question.

**Recommendation / proposal.** Question: *What should we do?* The answer is an action with a stated effect. The body answers *Why this?* and *How would it work?* — usually reasons first, plan second.

**Progress review / steering update.** Situation: the plan as previously agreed. Complication: what has happened since — ahead, behind, or changed. Question: *Where are we, and what needs deciding?* The governing thought should carry the judgement ("On track except workstream three, which needs a scope decision today"), never a neutral tour of workstreams. A progress review with no decision to make and no judgement to convey is a calendar entry, not a document.

**Request for resources.** Situation: the objective the reader already owns. Complication: the objective cannot be met with current resources, or an opportunity exceeds them. Question: *Should we fund this?* The answer states the ask and the return together ("£250k over two quarters returns the ability to serve the enterprise segment, worth an estimated £1.1m annually"). Burying the number is the classic failure; the reader spends the whole document braced for it.

**Diagnostic / "why is this happening" report.** Situation: the expected state. Complication: the observed deviation. Question: *What is causing it?* The governing thought names the cause and, usually, gates the fix ("The backlog is driven by rework from a single upstream data feed; fixing the feed clears it within a quarter"). Resist the urge to structure the body as a chronicle of the investigation — structure it by causes considered, ordered by contribution.

**Options paper.** Question: *Which route should we take?* Two honest shapes exist. If you have a recommendation, lead with it and treat the alternatives as tested-and-rejected beneath it. If the decision genuinely rests with the reader, the governing thought states the decision criteria and what the choice turns on ("The options separate on one variable: how much integration risk we will carry for speed"). What an options paper must never do is present three options with no view at all — the writer always knows something the neutral table hides.

**Terms-of-reference response / commissioned work.** The Question was handed to you. Restate it in the introduction *as commissioned*, then answer it. If the work revealed that the commissioned question was the wrong one, say so in the governing thought — do not silently answer a different question and leave the reader to notice.

## 5. Vertical logic: the question-answer dialogue

Once the governing thought is stated, the reader's mind responds with a question — usually *Why?*, *How?*, or *Which ones?* The layer below must answer exactly that question, and nothing else. Each idea in that layer provokes its own question, answered by the layer below it, and so on until the questions stop — at which point the pyramid is complete and the document can end.

The test at every level: **write the idea, then say aloud the question a sceptical reader would ask on reading it.** If the ideas beneath do not answer that question, they belong elsewhere (or nowhere). If the reader would ask a question that nothing beneath answers, there is a gap.

Two failure modes are worth naming:

- **Answering an unasked question.** Detail that is accurate, interesting, and irrelevant to the question the parent provokes. It reads as padding because, structurally, it is. The commonest source is attachment to completed analysis.
- **Provoking a question you never answer.** A claim like "the pilot outperformed expectations" provokes *by how much, against what expectation?* If the layer beneath talks about something else, the reader carries the unanswered question through the rest of the document as an itch.

*Example.* Governing thought: "We should insource last-mile delivery in the metro regions." Reader's question: *Why?* Supporting layer: (a) contracted carriers miss the delivery window twice as often as the in-house pilot fleet; (b) insourced cost per drop reaches parity at volumes we already exceed in metro regions; (c) the pilot demonstrated we can recruit and retain drivers at the required rate. Each of these then provokes *How do you know?* — answered by the evidence layer beneath.

Vertical logic is what makes a document skimmable at any depth. A reader who accepts a claim skips its support; a reader who doubts it descends one layer. Nobody is forced to read detail they do not need.

## 6. Horizontal logic: deduction and induction

Ideas within a single group must relate to one another in one of two ways, and the choice between them is one of the most consequential structural decisions in a document.

### Deduction: the chain

A deductive grouping is a chain of argument: a premise about the world, a second premise that comments on or applies to the first, and a conclusion that follows from the two together. *"All regional carriers are repricing at renewal. Our carrier contracts renew in Q2. Therefore we should model repricing scenarios now."*

Deduction's strength is force: if the premises hold, the conclusion is compelled. Its weaknesses in business writing are practical:

- **It makes the reader wait.** The conclusion — the thing the reader cares about — arrives last, after premises whose relevance is not yet clear.
- **It is fragile.** A reader who disputes any link rejects the whole chain, and readers dispute premises constantly.
- **Chains compound badly.** A three-step argument where each step is itself supported by a three-step argument forces the reader to hold nine linked claims before anything resolves. Long deductive structures are how documents become unreadable while remaining technically correct.

Deduction has a legitimate home in the *lower* levels of the pyramid, where a specific inference needs to be nailed down rigorously, and in genuinely logical subject matter (legal analysis, eligibility tests, compliance gates). Keep any chain to a few steps, and state where it is heading before walking it.

### Induction: the set

An inductive grouping is a set of like items that jointly support a general claim: three reasons, four problems, five options. The parent idea names what the items have in common and what they add up to.

Induction's strength is robustness and speed: the reader gets the conclusion immediately, sees the shape of the support ("three reasons"), and can weigh each item independently — rejecting one does not collapse the claim. Its demand is discipline: the items must genuinely be the same kind of thing (the test: can you name the plural noun that covers all of them — *reasons*, *steps*, *symptoms*?), and the leap from items to claim must be warranted (see §9).

### Converting deduction to induction

Most deductive arguments can be re-presented inductively, and at the top of a pyramid they usually should be. The manoeuvre: state the deductive *conclusion* as the parent idea, then present the premises as parallel supporting points beneath it — reframed as reasons rather than steps.

*Deductive:* market repricing is coming → our contracts renew in Q2 → therefore model scenarios now.
*Inductive presentation:* **"We should model repricing scenarios this month"** supported by (a) the market context: regional carriers are repricing at renewal; (b) our exposure: our contracts renew in Q2; (c) the lead time: scenario modelling takes six weeks.

Same logic, but the reader gets the action first and can probe the support in any order. A useful default: **argue deductively in your analysis if the logic demands it; present inductively on the page**, especially in the top two layers, where senior readers live.

### Grouping fallacies

Whichever mode you use, three fallacies recur:

- **False sameness** — items that share a topic but not a kind ("improve morale" sitting beside "the Q3 attrition figure": an action and a fact). Topic overlap is not logical sameness.
- **The smuggled premise** — a deductive chain whose conclusion depends on a premise never stated, usually because the writer considers it obvious. Sceptical readers exist to find these.
- **The unearned generalisation** — an inductive parent that claims more than the items support: three anecdotes summarised as "clients are demanding X". The fix is either better evidence or a more modest parent.

## 7. MECE: the grouping discipline

A well-formed inductive group is **mutually exclusive** (items do not overlap — no fact belongs in two boxes) and **collectively exhaustive** (together the items cover the ground — nothing material is left out). The acronym MECE, which Minto coined, names this standard.

- **Overlap** breeds double-counting and muddled ownership. If "improve digital onboarding" and "reduce time-to-first-transaction" appear as sibling items, the reader cannot tell where onboarding friction belongs.
- **Gaps** breed unearned conclusions. If a cost review covers staff, property, and technology but not third-party spend, the claim "we have identified all material savings" is not supported.

Practical tests:

1. Can each item be defined so that no fact could sit under two of them?
2. Does the set answer the parent question completely, so that "anything else?" draws a confident no?
3. Are there three to five items? Two suggests a false dichotomy or an incomplete cut; six or more suggests the group needs an intermediate layer.

Where do MECE cuts come from? Rarely from staring at a blank page. The reliable sources are:

- **The structure of the thing itself** — an organisation's divisions, a process's stages, a P&L's lines. Structural cuts inherit their exhaustiveness from reality.
- **An algebraic identity** — revenue = customers × frequency × value per transaction; any driver of the left side must live in exactly one factor on the right. Identities are MECE by construction and are the workhorse of diagnostic trees (§11).
- **A genuine dichotomy and its residue** — internal vs. external, regulated vs. unregulated, within our control vs. not. Safe when the dividing test is crisp.
- **Received frameworks** — used with care. A borrowed framework is only MECE *for your question* if its categories actually partition your problem; forcing material into a framework built for a different question produces tidy-looking gaps.

MECE is a discipline, not a fetish. The goal is a cut of the problem that a sceptical reader cannot pick apart, achieved with the smallest workable number of categories.

## 8. Ordering ideas within a group

Within any inductive group, the sequence must reflect the logic by which the group was formed. Three orders cover almost every case:

- **Time order** — for steps in a process or events in a sequence. First things first. *Diagnose → design → pilot → scale.*
- **Structural order** — for parts of a whole. Follow the structure of the thing itself: geography north to south, a balance sheet top to bottom, an organisation by division. The order should mirror a structure the reader can visualise.
- **Ranking order** — for items of the same class that vary in strength or size. Most important first. *The largest cost driver, then the second, then the third.*

The deeper point: order is *diagnostic*. If none of the three orders fits, the group has probably not been properly formed — the items may not be the same kind of thing. Reordering a ragged list often reveals that two items are really one, or that one belongs in a different branch. Conversely, when a document's sections could be shuffled without anyone noticing, that is not flexibility; it is evidence that no ordering logic exists, which usually means no grouping logic exists either.

One practical note on ranking order: "most important first" means most important *to the parent claim*, not most interesting to the writer or most recent to happen. The item that does the most work supporting the parent leads.

## 9. Summarising a group: earn the parent idea

The weakest habit in business writing is the **intellectually blank assertion**: a parent statement that labels its children without summarising them. "The company faces three challenges" tells the reader nothing; it merely promises a list. The parent idea must state what the three challenges *amount to*: "The company's three biggest challenges all stem from underinvestment in mid-office controls."

Blank assertions are seductive because they are always true — a list of three things is indeed three things — and because they postpone the hard part. But they cost the document its cumulative force. If every parent is a label, the top of the pyramid is a label too, and the document has no message. The reader finishes knowing what topics were covered and nothing else.

To earn a summary, first identify what kind of ideas the group contains:

**Action ideas (steps, recommendations, initiatives).** Summarise by stating the *effect* of carrying out the actions together. Not "take the following five actions" but "these five actions cut onboarding time from twelve days to three." Two disciplines:

- *State each action at the level of its intended effect, concretely enough to be verified.* "Improve communication" summarises nothing and could contain anything; "give account teams a weekly view of open service issues" can be done, checked, and costed.
- *Group actions by the effect they jointly produce, not by department or theme.* Actions that together produce one outcome belong under that outcome — that outcome is the parent. When actions cause one another (A enables B enables C), what you have is a chain, and the summary is the end state the chain reaches.

**Situation ideas (findings, facts, observations).** Summarise by stating the *inference* the facts jointly support. Not "we observed the following" but "the evidence points to pricing, not service, as the cause of attrition." The test: what may I conclude *because* all of these are true, that I could not conclude from any one alone? If the honest answer is "nothing", the grouping is decorative — the facts share a topic but support no common inference, and the group should be dissolved and its members reassigned to parents they actually serve.

**The summary as discovery.** Forcing yourself to earn each parent is not a writing chore; it is where the remaining thinking happens. Three findings that resist summary usually mean a fourth finding is missing, or one of the three is really evidence for a different claim, or the honest inference is weaker than the document's thrust assumes. Each of these is better discovered by the writer than the reader. In practice, the moment a document "clicks" is almost always the moment a stubborn group finally yields its true parent — and the parent turns out to be more interesting than the label it replaced.

# Part II — Thinking before writing

## 10. Defining the problem

Documents go wrong most often before a word is written, by answering the wrong question. Before building the pyramid, define the problem explicitly:

1. **Starting point.** Where does the reader's organisation stand, in the domain at issue? Sketch the structure: the process, market, or system within which the trouble arose.
2. **Disturbance.** What has happened, or is anticipated, that makes the status quo untenable or the opportunity live? Something changed, failed, threatened, or appeared — otherwise there is no document to write.
3. **Current outcome (R1).** What result does the reader have now, stated concretely?
4. **Desired outcome (R2).** What result does the reader want instead, stated concretely enough that arrival would be recognisable? A vague R2 ("a stronger digital proposition") guarantees a vague document; insist on the observable version ("digital channel handling 60% of routine service volume").
5. **The question.** Given the gap between R1 and R2, what is the reader actually asking?

The question the R1–R2 gap generates varies with where the reader stands, and each variant produces a different document:

- **They don't know how to get to R2** → *How do we get there?* — a solution paper.
- **They have a proposed route and want it validated** → *Is this the right way?* — an appraisal.
- **They know the route but not how to implement it** → *How do we make it work?* — an implementation plan.
- **They don't yet know why R1 arose** → *What is causing this?* — a diagnostic, which must come before any solution paper (a solution to an undiagnosed problem is a guess).
- **They face several routes** → *Which one?* — an options paper.
- **They suspect R2 itself is wrong** → *Should we want something else?* — a strategy question, the rarest and most valuable to catch, because every other document type inherits its answer.

Getting this wrong is expensive in a specific way: a beautifully structured answer to the wrong question is *harder* to challenge than a messy one, because its internal logic is sound. The reader senses something is off but cannot locate it. Always confirm the question — ideally with the reader — before structuring the answer.

The problem definition also generates the introduction directly: starting point and disturbance become the Situation and Complication; the question becomes the Question; the document's governing thought is the Answer. If you cannot fill in the five slots, you are not ready to write — you are ready to go and find out.

## 11. Structuring the analysis: logic trees and hypotheses

The pyramid structures the *presentation* of thinking. Its counterpart during the analysis itself is the **logic tree**: a top-down decomposition of the problem into parts that can be investigated separately. The two are siblings — both are hierarchies governed by MECE — but they run in opposite directions. The logic tree breaks a question down into work; the pyramid builds findings up into a message. Doing the tree well is what makes the pyramid easy later, because the findings arrive pre-grouped.

### Diagnostic trees: finding the cause

When the question is *why is this happening*, decompose the outcome into the components that produce it, and locate the deviation:

- **Quantitative decomposition** rides an identity. Revenue is down → revenue = active customers × transactions per customer × value per transaction → which factor moved? Each factor decomposes again (active customers = openings − closures; closures by segment; and so on) until the deviation is cornered in a component small enough to investigate directly. Because identities are MECE by construction, nothing can hide between branches.
- **Process decomposition** rides a flow. Output is late → walk the stages of the process in order, measuring where time or volume is lost. The structure of the process guarantees exhaustiveness.
- **Comparative isolation** rides a difference. The problem appears in region A but not region B → list what differs between them; each difference is a candidate cause to test.

The discipline in all three: follow the numbers down the tree and let them close branches. A branch whose component didn't move is finished, however interesting it looked.

### Solution trees: generating the options

When the question is *how do we get to R2*, decompose the goal into the levers that could move it. "Reduce cost per claim" splits into reduce claims volume / reduce cost per claim handled; each splits again (volume: prevention, deflection, fraud; unit cost: automation, supplier terms, rework elimination) until the leaves are individual actions that can be sized. A MECE solution tree is a completeness proof for an options paper: it shows the recommendation was chosen from the full space, not from the first three ideas in the room.

### Hypothesis-led work

Trees can be explored exhaustively or steered by hypothesis. The hypothesis-led approach: form an early, explicit best guess at the answer; identify the smallest set of analyses that would confirm or kill it; run those first. Its virtues are speed and focus — you analyse what matters to the conclusion rather than everything that could be known. Its danger is attachment: a hypothesis defended is confirmation bias with a project plan. Two safeguards:

- **Design the killing test, not the confirming one.** Ask what evidence would make you abandon the hypothesis, and go get that evidence first.
- **Hold the tree alongside the hypothesis.** The tree shows which branches your hypothesis ignores; if it dies, you re-enter the tree at the point of failure rather than starting over.

### From tree to pyramid

When the analysis ends, the tree does not become the document. The tree is organised by *question asked*; the pyramid is organised by *answer found*. Branches that closed without incident disappear or compress to a line ("volume and mix were flat; the entire deviation is price"). The branch where the answer lives expands into the document's spine. Writers who skip this conversion produce the "journey document" — a faithful tour of everything investigated, in the order investigated — which is the most common structural failure in analytical writing and the direct result of presenting the tree instead of the pyramid.

# Part III — Delivery

## 12. From pyramid to page

The pyramid is the plan; the page must reveal it.

- **Headings mirror the structure.** Top-level sections correspond to the top layer of the pyramid; subsections to the layer beneath. Headings state ideas, not topics: "Consolidate around four hub depots", not "Depot analysis". A reader should be able to reconstruct the argument from the contents table alone.
- **State the point, then support it.** Every section and every paragraph opens with its claim. Detail follows; it never precedes.
- **Signpost the group.** When introducing a set of supporting ideas, say how many there are and what they jointly establish, so the reader knows the shape of what is coming.
- **Write transitions as logic, not filler.** The link between sections is the reasoning ("Because the savings depend on the delivery promise holding, the next question is service risk"), not throat-clearing ("Having discussed costs, we now turn to service"). A transition that references the *idea* of the previous section, rather than its existence, proves the sections connect.
- **One idea per paragraph.** If a paragraph contains two ideas, it is two paragraphs or one badly formed idea.
- **Depth is optional; the spine is not.** Push detail downwards — appendices, footnotes, linked workings — so the main text stays at the altitude of the argument. The test of a well-built document: reading only headings and opening sentences yields the complete argument, with everything beneath serving as optional depth.

## 13. Slides and presentations

A slide deck is a pyramid delivered a branch at a time, with the presenter supplying the vertical logic aloud. The same rules apply, plus constraints unique to the medium.

**The deck's spine is the titles.** Each slide title is one idea from the pyramid, written as a full claim — "Attrition is concentrated in year-two customers", never "Attrition analysis". Read in sequence, the titles alone should deliver the entire argument; this "title test" is the deck equivalent of the headings test, and decks that fail it are collections of exhibits, not arguments.

**One idea per slide, and the body proves the title.** Everything on the slide exists to support its title claim. Evidence that supports a different claim belongs on a different slide, however conveniently it fits the current chart. If a slide needs two titles, it is two slides.

**Choose the exhibit after the claim, not before.** The chart type follows from what the title asserts: a comparison claim wants magnitudes side by side; a trend claim wants a time series; a composition claim wants the parts of the whole; a relationship claim wants the two variables plotted against each other. Building the deck from available charts — then hunting for titles — inverts this and produces exhibit-driven arguments, which sprawl.

**Structure slides carry the pyramid's joints.** An agenda slide states the governing thought and the three-to-five supports (not a list of topics); tracker slides at each transition re-show the structure with the current branch highlighted, so the audience always knows where in the argument they stand. In a document, headings do this silently; in a live presentation, the audience cannot flip back, so the structure must be re-shown.

**Text slides are for the argument's joints, not its prose.** Where a document would persuade in paragraphs, a deck states the claim in the title, shows the minimum evidence, and lets the presenter argue. A deck that reads fully without a presenter is a document in landscape; if it must circulate standalone, write the missing vertical logic into a note line or an accompanying page rather than crowding the slides.

**The storyboard comes first.** Write every title, in order, on one page before building any slide. The storyboard is the pyramid flattened into a sequence, and it is where structural faults are cheap to fix. Amending the argument after thirty slides exist is how decks end up with the structure of the *first* draft of the thinking.

## 14. Short-form: emails and one-pagers

The method compresses; it does not switch off.

**Emails.** The subject line is the governing thought ("Recommend we delay the release to 14 March"), not the topic ("Release planning"). The first sentence restates it with the key reason. Supports follow as a short list, each one line, ordered by importance. The ask — who must do what by when — is explicit and placed where scanning finds it (first or last, never buried mid-paragraph). If the email needs SCQA, it fits in two sentences: one for situation-plus-complication, one for the answer.

**One-pagers.** A one-pager is the top two layers of the pyramid and nothing else: governing thought, the three-to-five supports each with one or two lines of evidence, the decision requested. Its discipline is refusing the third layer — detail goes to an appendix or a linked document. The strongest one-pagers are written *after* the full analysis, as its summit, not before it as a preview of intentions.

**Meeting pre-reads.** State the decision sought at the top, then the recommendation, then the minimum support. The pre-read's job is to move the meeting from information transfer to decision; a pre-read structured as background material guarantees the meeting re-performs the analysis.

# Part IV — Worked examples

## 15. Worked example A: a memo, before and after

*Fictional scenario: Kestrel Foods, a regional food manufacturer. An operations analyst has reviewed why the new Ashford production line has not delivered its promised output. Both versions below are invented in full.*

### Before: the journey memo

> **Subject: Ashford Line 4 — review findings**
>
> As requested, we have completed our review of Line 4 performance. We began by examining the original business case, which assumed output of 44,000 units per week by month six. We then collected run data for months one to nine and interviewed the shift managers and the maintenance team.
>
> Output in month nine was 31,200 units per week. We looked first at planned downtime, which is in line with the business case. Unplanned downtime, however, is running at 14% against an assumption of 6%. We examined the maintenance logs and found that 60% of unplanned stoppages relate to the infeed conveyor, which was retained from the old line rather than replaced. The conveyor was outside the scope of the capital project for budget reasons.
>
> We also reviewed staffing. The business case assumed experienced operators, but four of the nine operators are new hires still in training, which the shift managers believe slows changeovers. Changeover times average 48 minutes against a target of 30. We additionally considered raw material quality, seasonal demand patterns, and the scheduling system, none of which showed material issues.
>
> There are several possible ways forward, including replacing the conveyor, accelerating operator training, and revisiting the output target. We would be happy to discuss these options.

The reader must do all the work: the answer (such as it is) never arrives, the material is ordered by the sequence of the investigation, cleared branches get as much space as the cause, and the memo ends by offering a meeting instead of a recommendation.

### After: the pyramid memo

> **Subject: Ashford Line 4 will hit its output target only if we replace the infeed conveyor — recommend approving the £180k replacement now**
>
> Line 4 was built to reach 44,000 units per week by month six; at month nine it is delivering 31,200. Our review found the shortfall has one dominant cause and one secondary cause, both fixable: **replacing the legacy infeed conveyor and completing operator training recovers the full target within four months.**
>
> **1. The conveyor is the dominant cause — worth ~9,000 of the 12,800 missing units.** Unplanned downtime is 14% against a 6% assumption, and 60% of stoppages trace to the infeed conveyor retained from the old line (excluded from the original project for budget reasons). At quoted cost of £180k and current contribution margins, replacement pays back in under five months.
>
> **2. Operator inexperience is the secondary cause — worth ~3,000 units.** Four of nine operators are mid-training, and changeovers average 48 minutes against a 30-minute target. The existing training plan closes this by month thirteen at no additional cost; no intervention is needed beyond protecting the plan.
>
> **3. Nothing else material is wrong.** Planned downtime, raw material quality, scheduling, and demand patterns all match business-case assumptions — the target itself remains sound.
>
> **Decision requested:** approval of the £180k conveyor replacement at the March capital committee. With approval, we forecast full target run-rate by month thirteen.

Same facts, same investigation. The differences are entirely structural: the subject line is the governing thought; the shortfall is decomposed MECE (dominant cause / secondary cause / everything cleared) in ranking order; each section title is an earned claim with its quantified share of the gap; cleared branches compress to one line; and the memo ends with the decision it exists to obtain.

## 16. Worked example B: a recommendation, built from the problem definition up

*Fictional scenario: Harwood & Vale, a mid-sized accountancy firm, deciding whether to build an in-house data analytics team. This example shows the full build sequence: problem definition → introduction → pyramid.*

**Problem definition.**
- *Starting point:* advisory revenue has grown 30% in two years, delivered via subcontracted analysts.
- *Disturbance:* subcontractor rates up 50%; availability now constrains intake.
- *R1:* advisory growth capped by external capacity; ~£400k of work declined last year.
- *R2:* advisory capacity that scales with demand at acceptable cost.
- *Question:* should the firm build the capability in-house? (An appraisal of a proposed route — not a diagnostic; the cause of the constraint is known.)

**Introduction (generated directly from the definition).**
- *Situation:* Harwood & Vale's advisory revenue has grown 30% in two years, driven by clients asking for data-led insight alongside compliance work.
- *Complication:* that work is delivered through subcontractors whose costs have risen 50% and whose availability now constrains how much advisory work the firm can accept.
- *Question (implicit):* should the firm build the capability in-house?
- *Answer / governing thought:* **Harwood & Vale should build a six-person in-house analytics team over eighteen months, because doing so removes the growth constraint, pays back within two years, and is achievable with the firm's existing hiring channels.**

**Supporting layer** (inductive — three reasons, ranking order, each answering *Why?*):

1. **It removes the binding constraint on advisory growth.** Subcontractor availability capped advisory intake at an estimated £400k of declined work last year; an in-house team removes the cap. *(Beneath: pipeline analysis, declined-engagement log.)*
2. **The economics clear the firm's investment threshold.** At current advisory volumes, in-house delivery reaches cost parity with subcontracting in month 14 and pays back the build cost within two years. *(Beneath: cost model, sensitivity to volume downside.)*
3. **The build is executable.** The firm's graduate intake and one senior hire cover the skills required; two comparable regional firms have completed similar builds on the same timeline. *(Beneath: role design, market benchmarks.)*

Note the discipline at work: the governing thought summarises the three reasons rather than labelling them; the three reasons are the same kind of thing (reasons), do not overlap (demand, economics, feasibility), and jointly cover the grounds on which the decision turns; and each provokes exactly the question its sub-layer answers. Note also what the appraisal framing did: because the question was *is this the right way?*, the pyramid tests one route thoroughly rather than surveying all routes — the solution-tree survey happened earlier and appears only as a line in the body ("alternatives considered and set aside: permanent subcontract framework, acquisition of a boutique").

## 17. Worked example C: a slide deck storyboard, before and after

*Fictional scenario: Bramley General Insurance. A claims transformation team must present to the executive committee on rising motor claims costs. The storyboard is the deck reduced to its title sequence — the level at which structure is cheap to fix. Both versions are invented in full.*

### Before: the exhibit-driven storyboard

The team built the deck from the analyses they had completed. The titles, in order:

1. Agenda
2. Motor claims cost — five-year trend
3. Market benchmarking
4. Claims cost breakdown by category
5. Repair cost deep-dive
6. Parts price inflation
7. Labour rate analysis
8. Total loss frequency
9. Third-party claims analysis
10. Supplier panel overview
11. Process observations from site visits
12. Technology landscape
13. Options
14. Next steps

Apply the **title test** — read only the titles. What is the argument? There isn't one. Every title names a topic, so the sequence delivers no message; the audience must assemble the conclusion themselves from fourteen exhibits. The order is the order of the workstreams, not of any logic. There is no way to tell which slides matter (is slide 7 a cause or a cleared branch?), and the recommendation — presumably hiding in slide 13 — arrives cold after twelve slides of evidence for it, the deductive delivery at maximum length. This deck will run long, invite tangents on early exhibits, and reach its ask with five minutes left. Structurally, it is the tree presented instead of the pyramid (§11).

### After: the argument-driven storyboard

The team first wrote the governing thought, then the three supports, then re-selected exhibits to prove each claim. The titles, in order:

1. **Motor claims costs are up 22% in two years — three actions contain them without harming settlement quality** *(governing thought as agenda: the three supports listed beneath)*
2. **The entire increase sits in repair costs; frequency, total losses, and third-party costs are flat** *(the MECE decomposition — one slide retires four branches of the old deck)*
3. **Two-thirds of the repair increase is price, not volume: parts inflation and labour rates, both above market** *(the diagnosis, quantified)*
4. **We pay above benchmark because 60% of repairs flow to non-panel garages at uncontrolled rates** *(the cause behind the cause)*
5. **Action 1 — steer repairs to the panel: worth an estimated £6m annually** *(first action, sized; evidence beneath)*
6. **Action 2 — reprice the panel agreement at renewal: worth £3m** *(second action, sized)*
7. **Action 3 — pre-authorise parts substitution on repairs under £2,500: worth £2m, with settlement-quality safeguards** *(third action, sized, objection pre-answered)*
8. **Tracker: the three actions recover £11m of the £14m increase — what remains is market-wide inflation we should plan to absorb** *(honest residue: the claim is not overstated)*
9. **Decision requested today: approve panel-steering mandate and the renewal negotiating envelope** *(the ask, isolated on its own slide)*

Nine slides instead of fourteen, and the title test now passes: the titles alone are a complete, ordered argument — problem, diagnosis, cause, three sized actions, honest limit, ask. The decomposition slide (2) compresses every cleared branch into one exhibit rather than four. Each action slide's title carries its value, so ranking order is visible. The audience knows the ask from slide 1 and evaluates everything against it — the inductive delivery. The detail from the old slides 5–12 has not vanished; it has moved to appendix slides, pulled up only if a claim is challenged.

The general lesson: the two decks contain the same analysis. The first presents the *work*; the second presents the *answer*. Storyboarding first — all titles on one page before any slide is built — is what makes the second deck no more expensive to produce than the first.

# Part V — Working with others

## 18. Reviewing other people's documents

The method is as useful for reading as for writing: it gives a reviewer a repeatable way to locate *why* a document isn't working, instead of the unactionable "it doesn't flow." The protocol below takes ten to fifteen minutes and diagnoses most drafts.

### Step 1: Extract the skeleton

Copy out only the headings (or slide titles) and the first sentence of each section. This is the document's de facto pyramid — the structure the reader will actually experience, whatever the writer intended. Most structural faults are visible in the skeleton alone, and reviewing the skeleton keeps you off the prose, where reviewers habitually hide from the harder structural conversation.

### Step 2: Test the top

- **Find the governing thought.** Is there a single sentence that carries the document's message? If the candidate is a topic ("This paper reviews our pricing approach") rather than a claim, the document has a subject but no message — the most serious possible finding, and everything else waits on it.
- **Test it against the introduction.** Does the opening establish a Situation the reader accepts and a Complication that raises exactly the question the governing thought answers? The commonest fault here is the *writer's-situation opening* — a first paragraph narrating the team's activity — followed closely by the *question mismatch*, where the introduction sets up one question and the document answers another.

### Step 3: Test each group

For every set of sibling headings in the skeleton:

- **Same kind?** Can you name the plural noun that covers all the siblings — reasons, steps, findings, options? A group of "two findings, one recommendation, and some context" fails.
- **Earned parent?** Is the parent a summary of the siblings, or a label? Count the blank assertions ("Key considerations", "Three challenges") — each is a place where the thinking stopped early.
- **MECE?** Any two siblings the same fact could sit under? Any obvious question the set leaves uncovered?
- **Ordered?** Which of the three orders (time, structure, ranking) is this group using? If you can't tell, and shuffling the siblings would change nothing, flag it — an unshufflable order usually indicates an unformed group.

### Step 4: Test the vertical dialogue

Walk the skeleton top-down, saying aloud the question each claim provokes, and check the material beneath answers it. Mark the two failure modes: **orphaned detail** (sections answering questions nobody asked — often the writer's favourite analysis) and **hanging questions** (claims that provoke a question the document never answers). Both are cut/insert instructions, which makes them unusually easy feedback to act on.

### Step 5: Only now, the prose

Paragraph openings, transitions-as-logic, one idea per paragraph — the §12 disciplines. There is no point line-editing prose that sits in the wrong place; structure comments first, prose comments second, and if the structure needs rebuilding, hold the prose comments entirely.

### Giving the feedback

- **Lead with the question, not the verdict.** "What's the one thing you want the committee to walk away believing?" is more productive than "there's no governing thought" — and the writer's spoken answer is usually better than anything in the draft. Very often the review's main output is that sentence, captured and promoted to the top.
- **Feed back in pyramid order.** One governing observation about the draft, then the two or three structural points that support it, then detail on request. A review of a structure should model structure.
- **Distinguish the three layers.** Is the problem the *thinking* (wrong question, unearned conclusions — send it back to §10–11), the *structure* (right thinking, wrong shape — rebuild the skeleton together), or the *prose* (right shape, rough surface — line edits)? Misdiagnosing a thinking problem as a prose problem produces beautifully polished wrong answers, and it is the default failure of hurried reviews.
- **Review the storyboard, not the deck.** For presentations, ask for the title sequence before slides are built (§13). Ten titles on a page can be restructured in minutes; thirty finished slides defend themselves.

### The reviewer's checklist, compressed

1. Skeleton extracted?
2. Governing thought present, and a claim?
3. Introduction generates the question the document answers?
4. Every group: same kind, earned parent, MECE, ordered?
5. Vertical dialogue: no orphaned detail, no hanging questions?
6. Feedback delivered pyramid-first, thinking/structure/prose distinguished?

## 19. Pre-flight checklist

Before releasing any analytical document:

**Structure**
- [ ] Can the whole argument be read from the headings (or slide titles) and opening sentences alone?
- [ ] At every level, do the ideas below answer the exact question the idea above provokes — and is every provoked question answered?
- [ ] Is every group one kind of thing, non-overlapping, and complete for its parent question?
- [ ] Does every group follow a deliberate order — time, structure, or ranking?
- [ ] Are there three to five items in each group, with intermediate layers where there were more?

**Message**
- [ ] Does the introduction's Situation contain only what the reader already accepts?
- [ ] Does the governing thought answer the question the Complication raises — and is it a claim, not a topic?
- [ ] Is every parent idea an earned summary (effect of actions, or inference from facts) rather than a label?
- [ ] Is each action stated at the level of its effect, concretely enough to verify?

**Thinking**
- [ ] Was the problem defined (starting point, disturbance, R1, R2, question) before the structure was built — and confirmed with the reader where possible?
- [ ] Is the document organised by answer found, not by question asked — no journey narrative, no tour of cleared branches?
- [ ] For hypothesis-led work: was the killing test run, not just the confirming ones?

**Delivery**
- [ ] Does every paragraph (or slide) open with — and contain only — one idea?
- [ ] Are transitions written as logic, not filler?
- [ ] Is the ask (who decides what, by when) explicit and findable by a scanner?
