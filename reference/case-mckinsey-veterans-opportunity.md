# Case Study: McKinsey "How Companies Can Capture the Veteran Opportunity" (2012)

A real-world example of the Pyramid Principle executed at senior-consulting standard. This deck was commissioned by the White House Business Council and presented to Fortune 500 employers. It demonstrates *applied* pyramid structure in a business-to-business context.

## The Storyboard (Title Sequence)

Reading these titles in order delivers the entire argument without seeing any evidence:

1. **Cover** — How companies can capture the veteran opportunity
2. **Executive summary** — Veterans bring distinctive capabilities and valuable skills; employers can earn $9,600+ in tax credits; recruiting veterans builds goodwill with stakeholders
3. **Agenda** — *Three sections:* The business case for hiring veterans · Veterans opportunity overview · Best practices for hiring veterans · Available resources
4. **Why hire veterans?** — There is a compelling business case (section title + argument intro)
5. **High-performing companies recognize veteran value** — Real employers (AT&T, Amazon, GE, CSX) actively hire and retain veterans (proof: examples)
6. **Veterans bring distinctive capabilities** — Performance-oriented, teamwork, leadership, discipline, problem-solving (business case: soft skills)
7. **Veterans bring valuable technical skills** — Logistics, IT, medical, security, engineering, languages (business case: technical skills + reduced training cost)
8. **Employers earn $9,600+ federal and state tax credits per veteran** — Detail on credit size, eligibility, and claim process (business case: financial incentive)
9. **Hiring veterans builds goodwill** — Community, customers, and employees all perceive hiring veterans as the right thing to do (business case: goodwill case)
10. **Who are our veterans?** — Meet Alan: 24-year-old post-9/11 veteran with leadership, technical, and resilience experience (context: making the veteran tangible)
11. **Veterans are returning home and are an untapped resource** — 2.9M have returned; 18–24-year-old vets 2× more likely to be unemployed than peers; nearly 71% willing to relocate (market opportunity: supply-side)
12. **More veteran talent will be available in coming years** — Defense budget cuts forcing troop reductions; higher supply ahead (market opportunity: timing)
13. **Employers who help veterans transition will capitalize most effectively** — Bridge statement connecting supply to execution (transition: problem to solution)
14. **Agenda tracker** — Highlighting "Best practices" section (structural signpost)
15. **Four guiding principles for veteran recruiting** — Tie to business strategy · Build understanding and commitment · Leverage network · Develop compelling reason (execution: framework)
16. **Best practices for finding, hiring, onboarding, and retaining veterans** — 16 specific practices mapped to each lifecycle stage (execution: playbook)
17. **Foundational activities support later recruiting efforts** — Strategy phase activities (determine needs, enlist executives, build core team, develop EVP) (execution: sequencing)
18. **Amazon's efforts successfully support strategic objectives** — Case study showing opportunity-practice-result alignment (execution: proof of concept)
19. **Agenda tracker** — Back to full agenda (structural close)

## Pyramid Structure

```
GOVERNING THOUGHT (Slide 3, Executive Summary)
├─ Key-Line Argument 1: COMPELLING BUSINESS CASE
│  ├─ Veterans bring distinctive soft skills (performance, teamwork, leadership)
│  ├─ Veterans bring valuable technical skills (logistics, IT, medical, engineering)
│  └─ Tax incentives: $9,600+ per employee + goodwill benefits
│
├─ Key-Line Argument 2: UNTAPPED MARKET OPPORTUNITY
│  ├─ 2.9M have returned; supply growing; 71% willing to relocate
│  ├─ Young vets (18–24) 2× unemployment rate of peers
│  └─ Defense budget cuts create larger supply wave ahead
│
└─ Key-Line Argument 3: EXECUTABLE BEST PRACTICES EXIST
   ├─ Four guiding principles (tie to strategy, build commitment, leverage network, develop EVP)
   ├─ 16 specific practices across recruiting lifecycle
   └─ Proof: Amazon case study (opportunity + practices = 25% of fulfillment hires are vets)
```

**MECE test:** ✅ Why hire (business case) / When to hire (market supply) / How to hire (practices) — no overlap, complete coverage.

**Ordering:** ✅ Ranking by audience needs: first convince WHY (business case is strongest lever), then establish WHEN (supply is growing), then provide HOW (practices + proof).

**Answer-first:** ✅ Governing thought on slide 3; key-line on agenda slide 4; evidence flows from there.

## Why This Works as a Teaching Example

**1. Title test passes.** Reading slides 4–18 (skipping the trackers) in sequence tells the entire story:
- *Why hire veterans?* (business case)
- *Here's proof high-performing companies do it* (credibility)
- *Here are the capabilities and financial upsides* (detail)
- *Who are these people and why now?* (market context)
- *Here's the framework and practices* (execution)
- *Here's it working at Amazon* (validation)

No reader is lost; no evidence appears out of order.

**2. Earned summaries.** Every parent-level title is a summary, not a label:
- ❌ *Not* "Business case overview" → ✅ *Actually* "Veterans bring distinctive capabilities"
- ❌ *Not* "Market opportunity" → ✅ *Actually* "Veterans are returning home and are an untapped resource"
- ❌ *Not* "Best practices" → ✅ *Actually* "Employers who help veterans transition will capitalize most effectively"

Each summary *states what the evidence amounts to*, forcing McKinsey's team to earn every parent claim.

**3. One comparison per slide.** Each evidence slide makes one claim:
- Slide 6 claims soft skills; slides 7–8 claim technical skills; slide 9 claims financial incentives — three distinct facts under the one business-case umbrella.
- Slides 11–12 together claim the market opportunity is both *large and growing*.

No slide does triple duty; no evidence serves two masters.

**4. Structural markers (tracker slides).** Slides 4, 14, and 19 re-show the agenda with the current section highlighted. This is the live equivalent of your ability to flip back to the TOC — McKinsey was being read *aloud to an auditorium*, so trackers re-anchor the audience on where in the argument they stand.

**5. One question, one answer.** The entire deck answers one question: *How can companies capture the veteran opportunity?* Not two; not "should they and how" (two questions). The SCQA on slide 3 sets up exactly one question, and every slide from there is in service of that single answer.

## Structure Violations to Watch

The deck contains *one* structural choice that deviates from pure pyramid:
- **Slide 10 (Alan persona).** This slide introduces a human example mid-argument to make the veteran tangible. It's not *evidence* for any of the three business-case claims; it's a *narrative device* that resets the reader's attention before moving into the market opportunity section. This is legitimate, but it's an exception — most evidence slides answer the parent claim directly.

## Use in Practice

**For teaching:** Show slides 4–9 to demonstrate how McKinsey structures the business case (three MECE capabilities). Then show slides 11–12 to demonstrate market-size arguments (volume + growth). Finally, slides 15–18 show the playbook structure (framework + detail + proof).

**For LLM instruction:** This storyboard makes a good test case for Copilot. Give the agent slides 4–18 (the titles only) and ask it to identify the pyramid: the governing thought, the key-line arguments, and the evidence structure. Copilot should be able to reverse-engineer the logic from titles alone.

**Against self-correction:** If you build a deck and the title test fails — reading titles alone doesn't tell the story — use this case as a reference. Every slide title here is a complete sentence that advances the argument.
