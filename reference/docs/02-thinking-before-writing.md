# Part II — Thinking before writing

## 10. Defining the problem

Documents go wrong most often before a word is written, by answering the wrong question. Before building the pyramid, define the problem explicitly:

1. **Starting point.** Where does the reader's organisation stand, in the domain at issue? Sketch the structure: the process, market, or system within which the trouble arose.
2. **Disturbance.** What has happened, or is anticipated, that makes the status quo untenable or the opportunity live? Something changed, failed, threatened, or appeared — otherwise there is no document to write.
3. **Current outcome (R1).** What result does the reader have now, stated concretely?
4. **Desired outcome (R2).** What result does the reader want instead, stated concretely enough that arrival would be recognisable? A vague R2 ("a stronger digital proposition") guarantees a vague document; insist on the observable version ("digital channel handling 60% of routine service volume").
5. **The question.** Given the gap between R1 and R2, what is the reader actually asking?

The question the R1–R2 gap generates varies with where the reader stands, and each variant produces a different document:

- **They don't know how to get to R2** → *How do we get there?* — a solution paper.
- **They have a proposed route and want it validated** → *Is this the right way?* — an appraisal.
- **They know the route but not how to implement it** → *How do we make it work?* — an implementation plan.
- **They don't yet know why R1 arose** → *What is causing this?* — a diagnostic, which must come before any solution paper (a solution to an undiagnosed problem is a guess).
- **They face several routes** → *Which one?* — an options paper.
- **They suspect R2 itself is wrong** → *Should we want something else?* — a strategy question, the rarest and most valuable to catch, because every other document type inherits its answer.

Getting this wrong is expensive in a specific way: a beautifully structured answer to the wrong question is *harder* to challenge than a messy one, because its internal logic is sound. The reader senses something is off but cannot locate it. Always confirm the question — ideally with the reader — before structuring the answer.

The problem definition also generates the introduction directly: starting point and disturbance become the Situation and Complication; the question becomes the Question; the document's governing thought is the Answer. If you cannot fill in the five slots, you are not ready to write — you are ready to go and find out.

## 11. Structuring the analysis: logic trees and hypotheses

The pyramid structures the *presentation* of thinking. Its counterpart during the analysis itself is the **logic tree**: a top-down decomposition of the problem into parts that can be investigated separately. The two are siblings — both are hierarchies governed by MECE — but they run in opposite directions. The logic tree breaks a question down into work; the pyramid builds findings up into a message. Doing the tree well is what makes the pyramid easy later, because the findings arrive pre-grouped.

### Diagnostic trees: finding the cause

When the question is *why is this happening*, decompose the outcome into the components that produce it, and locate the deviation:

- **Quantitative decomposition** rides an identity. Revenue is down → revenue = active customers × transactions per customer × value per transaction → which factor moved? Each factor decomposes again (active customers = openings − closures; closures by segment; and so on) until the deviation is cornered in a component small enough to investigate directly. Because identities are MECE by construction, nothing can hide between branches.
- **Process decomposition** rides a flow. Output is late → walk the stages of the process in order, measuring where time or volume is lost. The structure of the process guarantees exhaustiveness.
- **Comparative isolation** rides a difference. The problem appears in region A but not region B → list what differs between them; each difference is a candidate cause to test.

The discipline in all three: follow the numbers down the tree and let them close branches. A branch whose component didn't move is finished, however interesting it looked.

### Solution trees: generating the options

When the question is *how do we get to R2*, decompose the goal into the levers that could move it. "Reduce cost per claim" splits into reduce claims volume / reduce cost per claim handled; each splits again (volume: prevention, deflection, fraud; unit cost: automation, supplier terms, rework elimination) until the leaves are individual actions that can be sized. A MECE solution tree is a completeness proof for an options paper: it shows the recommendation was chosen from the full space, not from the first three ideas in the room.

### Hypothesis-led work

Trees can be explored exhaustively or steered by hypothesis. The hypothesis-led approach: form an early, explicit best guess at the answer; identify the smallest set of analyses that would confirm or kill it; run those first. Its virtues are speed and focus — you analyse what matters to the conclusion rather than everything that could be known. Its danger is attachment: a hypothesis defended is confirmation bias with a project plan. Two safeguards:

- **Design the killing test, not the confirming one.** Ask what evidence would make you abandon the hypothesis, and go get that evidence first.
- **Hold the tree alongside the hypothesis.** The tree shows which branches your hypothesis ignores; if it dies, you re-enter the tree at the point of failure rather than starting over.

### From tree to pyramid

When the analysis ends, the tree does not become the document. The tree is organised by *question asked*; the pyramid is organised by *answer found*. Branches that closed without incident disappear or compress to a line ("volume and mix were flat; the entire deviation is price"). The branch where the answer lives expands into the document's spine. Writers who skip this conversion produce the "journey document" — a faithful tour of everything investigated, in the order investigated — which is the most common structural failure in analytical writing and the direct result of presenting the tree instead of the pyramid.
