# Part III — Delivery

## 12. From pyramid to page

The pyramid is the plan; the page must reveal it.

- **Headings mirror the structure.** Top-level sections correspond to the top layer of the pyramid; subsections to the layer beneath. Headings state ideas, not topics: "Consolidate around four hub depots", not "Depot analysis". A reader should be able to reconstruct the argument from the contents table alone.
- **State the point, then support it.** Every section and every paragraph opens with its claim. Detail follows; it never precedes.
- **Signpost the group.** When introducing a set of supporting ideas, say how many there are and what they jointly establish, so the reader knows the shape of what is coming.
- **Write transitions as logic, not filler.** The link between sections is the reasoning ("Because the savings depend on the delivery promise holding, the next question is service risk"), not throat-clearing ("Having discussed costs, we now turn to service"). A transition that references the *idea* of the previous section, rather than its existence, proves the sections connect.
- **One idea per paragraph.** If a paragraph contains two ideas, it is two paragraphs or one badly formed idea.
- **Depth is optional; the spine is not.** Push detail downwards — appendices, footnotes, linked workings — so the main text stays at the altitude of the argument. The test of a well-built document: reading only headings and opening sentences yields the complete argument, with everything beneath serving as optional depth.

## 13. Slides and presentations

A slide deck is a pyramid delivered a branch at a time, with the presenter supplying the vertical logic aloud. The same rules apply, plus constraints unique to the medium.

**The deck's spine is the titles.** Each slide title is one idea from the pyramid, written as a full claim — "Attrition is concentrated in year-two customers", never "Attrition analysis". Read in sequence, the titles alone should deliver the entire argument; this "title test" is the deck equivalent of the headings test, and decks that fail it are collections of exhibits, not arguments.

**One idea per slide, and the body proves the title.** Everything on the slide exists to support its title claim. Evidence that supports a different claim belongs on a different slide, however conveniently it fits the current chart. If a slide needs two titles, it is two slides.

**Choose the exhibit after the claim, not before.** The chart type follows from what the title asserts: a comparison claim wants magnitudes side by side; a trend claim wants a time series; a composition claim wants the parts of the whole; a relationship claim wants the two variables plotted against each other. Building the deck from available charts — then hunting for titles — inverts this and produces exhibit-driven arguments, which sprawl.

**Structure slides carry the pyramid's joints.** An agenda slide states the governing thought and the three-to-five supports (not a list of topics); tracker slides at each transition re-show the structure with the current branch highlighted, so the audience always knows where in the argument they stand. In a document, headings do this silently; in a live presentation, the audience cannot flip back, so the structure must be re-shown.

**Text slides are for the argument's joints, not its prose.** Where a document would persuade in paragraphs, a deck states the claim in the title, shows the minimum evidence, and lets the presenter argue. A deck that reads fully without a presenter is a document in landscape; if it must circulate standalone, write the missing vertical logic into a note line or an accompanying page rather than crowding the slides.

**The storyboard comes first.** Write every title, in order, on one page before building any slide. The storyboard is the pyramid flattened into a sequence, and it is where structural faults are cheap to fix. Amending the argument after thirty slides exist is how decks end up with the structure of the *first* draft of the thinking.

## 14. Short-form: emails and one-pagers

The method compresses; it does not switch off.

**Emails.** The subject line is the governing thought ("Recommend we delay the release to 14 March"), not the topic ("Release planning"). The first sentence restates it with the key reason. Supports follow as a short list, each one line, ordered by importance. The ask — who must do what by when — is explicit and placed where scanning finds it (first or last, never buried mid-paragraph). If the email needs SCQA, it fits in two sentences: one for situation-plus-complication, one for the answer.

**One-pagers.** A one-pager is the top two layers of the pyramid and nothing else: governing thought, the three-to-five supports each with one or two lines of evidence, the decision requested. Its discipline is refusing the third layer — detail goes to an appendix or a linked document. The strongest one-pagers are written *after* the full analysis, as its summit, not before it as a preview of intentions.

**Meeting pre-reads.** State the decision sought at the top, then the recommendation, then the minimum support. The pre-read's job is to move the meeting from information transfer to decision; a pre-read structured as background material guarantees the meeting re-performs the analysis.
