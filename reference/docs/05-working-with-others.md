# Part V — Working with others

## 18. Reviewing other people's documents

The method is as useful for reading as for writing: it gives a reviewer a repeatable way to locate *why* a document isn't working, instead of the unactionable "it doesn't flow." The protocol below takes ten to fifteen minutes and diagnoses most drafts.

### Step 1: Extract the skeleton

Copy out only the headings (or slide titles) and the first sentence of each section. This is the document's de facto pyramid — the structure the reader will actually experience, whatever the writer intended. Most structural faults are visible in the skeleton alone, and reviewing the skeleton keeps you off the prose, where reviewers habitually hide from the harder structural conversation.

### Step 2: Test the top

- **Find the governing thought.** Is there a single sentence that carries the document's message? If the candidate is a topic ("This paper reviews our pricing approach") rather than a claim, the document has a subject but no message — the most serious possible finding, and everything else waits on it.
- **Test it against the introduction.** Does the opening establish a Situation the reader accepts and a Complication that raises exactly the question the governing thought answers? The commonest fault here is the *writer's-situation opening* — a first paragraph narrating the team's activity — followed closely by the *question mismatch*, where the introduction sets up one question and the document answers another.

### Step 3: Test each group

For every set of sibling headings in the skeleton:

- **Same kind?** Can you name the plural noun that covers all the siblings — reasons, steps, findings, options? A group of "two findings, one recommendation, and some context" fails.
- **Earned parent?** Is the parent a summary of the siblings, or a label? Count the blank assertions ("Key considerations", "Three challenges") — each is a place where the thinking stopped early.
- **MECE?** Any two siblings the same fact could sit under? Any obvious question the set leaves uncovered?
- **Ordered?** Which of the three orders (time, structure, ranking) is this group using? If you can't tell, and shuffling the siblings would change nothing, flag it — an unshufflable order usually indicates an unformed group.

### Step 4: Test the vertical dialogue

Walk the skeleton top-down, saying aloud the question each claim provokes, and check the material beneath answers it. Mark the two failure modes: **orphaned detail** (sections answering questions nobody asked — often the writer's favourite analysis) and **hanging questions** (claims that provoke a question the document never answers). Both are cut/insert instructions, which makes them unusually easy feedback to act on.

### Step 5: Only now, the prose

Paragraph openings, transitions-as-logic, one idea per paragraph — the §12 disciplines. There is no point line-editing prose that sits in the wrong place; structure comments first, prose comments second, and if the structure needs rebuilding, hold the prose comments entirely.

### Giving the feedback

- **Lead with the question, not the verdict.** "What's the one thing you want the committee to walk away believing?" is more productive than "there's no governing thought" — and the writer's spoken answer is usually better than anything in the draft. Very often the review's main output is that sentence, captured and promoted to the top.
- **Feed back in pyramid order.** One governing observation about the draft, then the two or three structural points that support it, then detail on request. A review of a structure should model structure.
- **Distinguish the three layers.** Is the problem the *thinking* (wrong question, unearned conclusions — send it back to §10–11), the *structure* (right thinking, wrong shape — rebuild the skeleton together), or the *prose* (right shape, rough surface — line edits)? Misdiagnosing a thinking problem as a prose problem produces beautifully polished wrong answers, and it is the default failure of hurried reviews.
- **Review the storyboard, not the deck.** For presentations, ask for the title sequence before slides are built (§13). Ten titles on a page can be restructured in minutes; thirty finished slides defend themselves.

### The reviewer's checklist, compressed

1. Skeleton extracted?
2. Governing thought present, and a claim?
3. Introduction generates the question the document answers?
4. Every group: same kind, earned parent, MECE, ordered?
5. Vertical dialogue: no orphaned detail, no hanging questions?
6. Feedback delivered pyramid-first, thinking/structure/prose distinguished?

## 19. Pre-flight checklist

Before releasing any analytical document:

**Structure**
- [ ] Can the whole argument be read from the headings (or slide titles) and opening sentences alone?
- [ ] At every level, do the ideas below answer the exact question the idea above provokes — and is every provoked question answered?
- [ ] Is every group one kind of thing, non-overlapping, and complete for its parent question?
- [ ] Does every group follow a deliberate order — time, structure, or ranking?
- [ ] Are there three to five items in each group, with intermediate layers where there were more?

**Message**
- [ ] Does the introduction's Situation contain only what the reader already accepts?
- [ ] Does the governing thought answer the question the Complication raises — and is it a claim, not a topic?
- [ ] Is every parent idea an earned summary (effect of actions, or inference from facts) rather than a label?
- [ ] Is each action stated at the level of its effect, concretely enough to verify?

**Thinking**
- [ ] Was the problem defined (starting point, disturbance, R1, R2, question) before the structure was built — and confirmed with the reader where possible?
- [ ] Is the document organised by answer found, not by question asked — no journey narrative, no tour of cleared branches?
- [ ] For hypothesis-led work: was the killing test run, not just the confirming ones?

**Delivery**
- [ ] Does every paragraph (or slide) open with — and contain only — one idea?
- [ ] Are transitions written as logic, not filler?
- [ ] Is the ask (who decides what, by when) explicit and findable by a scanner?
