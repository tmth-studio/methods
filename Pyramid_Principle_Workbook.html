Pyramid Principle: Guided Workbook

Strategic analysis and presentation workflow — Copilot executes, you check

v2 — full Minto coverage: lint rules, MECE, vertical/horizontal logic, copy standards, chart selection method, slide anatomy. Companion to Pyramid_Methods_Comprehensive_Final.docx.

How this workbook works

Division of labour. Copilot does the production work at every step: analysis, drafting, structuring, chart specification. You do three things only:

  1. Paste the step's prompt into Copilot — each step has a ready-made prompt (purple box). Paste it with your inputs attached.
  2. Check the output against the review checklist (amber box) — every check is a specific Minto rule, not a vibe.
  3. Record the approved output in the write-in box — it becomes the input for the next step. Never advance with an unapproved output.

The gates are the method. Minto's value is not the pyramid diagram — it is the discipline of testing every element before building on it. A failed check means Copilot repairs and resubmits; it never means "close enough".

A1

Frame the problem

Clarify what you are solving for and what decision depends on it

Input: raw problem statement (email, meeting note, briefing) · Output: one-sentence problem frame

Copilot's process — follow in order

  1. Read the source material (paste emails, briefing extracts, meeting notes into the prompt).
  2. Extract four elements: symptom (the observable problem), evidence (numbers, timeframe, comparison baseline), decision (what choice hangs on this), reader (who decides).
  3. Distinguish symptom from cause. "Churn rose" is a symptom. "Pricing is wrong" is a hypothesis — Copilot must not smuggle a diagnosis into the frame.
  4. Quantify the stakes: what does the problem cost per year, and what does the pending decision cost if wrong?
  5. Write one sentence: [symptom + magnitude + timeframe]; we need to [identify X] before [decision + cost].
  6. Flag any missing element explicitly rather than inventing it.

Prompt to give Copilot

You are running Step A1 (Frame the Problem) of a Minto Pyramid analysis.

Source material: [PASTE emails / notes / briefing]

Do this:
1. Extract: symptom (observable, with numbers), evidence (data, timeframe,
   baseline), decision at stake (with cost), reader (who decides).
2. Do NOT diagnose. If the source proposes causes, list them separately as
   "candidate hypotheses for later" — keep them out of the frame.
3. Write a one-sentence problem frame:
   "[Symptom + magnitude + timeframe]; we need to identify [X] before
   [decision + cost]."
4. List anything missing that I need to supply. Do not invent data.

Output: the frame sentence, the four elements as a table, missing items.

Your review checklist

  • ✓ Symptom is observable and quantified (not an interpretation)
  • ✓ No diagnosis has leaked into the frame ("because of..." = fail)
  • ✓ The decision and its cost are explicit
  • ✓ The reader is named
  • ✓ Flagged gaps are real gaps — supply the data or accept the limitation

Reference example

Good: "SME churn has risen from 22% to 35% over three years (£6M annual revenue impact); we need to identify the root cause before committing £2M to the product roadmap."

Bad: "We have a churn problem because our prices are too high." (Diagnosis smuggled in; no decision; no stakes.)

GATE: frame is one sentence, symptom-only, decision + reader explicit

Next: A2 — Build Situation & Complication
A2

Build Situation & Complication

Establish the non-controversial baseline, then isolate what changed

Input: problem frame (A1) + available data · Output: Situation statement, Complication statement, evidence register

Copilot's process — follow in order

  1. Gather baseline facts from available sources: segment size, revenue, historical metrics, market context, what has stayed constant.
  2. Sort every fact into two piles: non-controversial (reader accepts without proof) and contested (needs evidence). Only the first pile can enter the Situation.
  3. Draft the Situation: 2–4 sentences, facts only, no "but"/"however", no judgment words (failing, weak, poor).
  4. Isolate the change: what is different now vs. the baseline? When did it start? What broke as a consequence?
  5. Draft the Complication: starts with "But", names the change and the damage with numbers, ends by implying (not stating) the open question. It must not propose a solution.
  6. Build the evidence register: every fact used, with source and date, in a two-column table (fact | source).
  7. Run the Nod Test on both: would the named reader nod at every sentence without asking for proof? Mark any sentence that fails.

Prompt to give Copilot

You are running Step A2 (Situation & Complication) of a Minto analysis.

Problem frame: [PASTE from A1]
Available data: [PASTE data / attach files]

Do this:
1. Sort all facts: non-controversial vs. contested. Only non-controversial
   facts may enter the Situation.
2. Draft Situation (2–4 sentences): facts only, no "but/however", no
   judgment words. Every claim must trace to the data provided.
3. Draft Complication (2–3 sentences): starts "But", names what changed and
   what broke, with numbers. NO solution, NO diagnosis.
4. Build an evidence register: fact | source | date.
5. Run the Nod Test: for each sentence, state whether [READER] would nod
   without proof. Flag failures and propose repairs.

Output: Situation, Complication, evidence register, Nod Test results.

Your review checklist

  • ✓ Situation: every sentence passes the Nod Test — you'd stake the meeting on the reader not objecting
  • ✓ Situation contains zero interpretation (adjectives like "strong/weak" = fail)
  • ✓ Complication follows from the Situation — the tension is felt, not asserted
  • ✓ Complication contains no solution and no cause claim
  • ✓ Evidence register: every number has a source you could produce in the meeting

Reference example (Fairy Food Mother)

Situation: "Prosus acquired JET with a mandate to replicate the iFood playbook. JET holds ~45% UK market share. Young families are 45% of the base. The business is repositioning around profitability."

Complication: "But young families sit at 42% 90-day retention despite the highest promotional spend of any segment. They respond to deals, then leave."

Note the shape: the Situation makes the Complication hurt. Profitability mandate + margin burned on a segment that does not retain = tension the reader feels before you say a word about solutions.

GATE: both statements pass the Nod Test; every fact sourced

Next: A3 — Generate and test hypotheses
A3

Generate and test hypotheses

Identify candidate causes, predict what each implies, test against data

Input: Situation + Complication + evidence register (A2) · Output: hypothesis table with verdicts, strongest hypothesis, research gaps

Copilot's process — follow in order

  1. Generate 3–5 candidate hypotheses for the root cause. Include the "obvious" explanation (the one stakeholders already believe) — it must be tested, not assumed.
  2. Check the set is MECE: hypotheses must not overlap (two phrasings of the same cause) and must plausibly cover the space (ask: "what ELSE could explain this?").
  3. For each hypothesis, write predictions: "If H is true, the data should show X, Y, Z." Predictions must be checkable with available or gatherable data.
  4. Critically: write disconfirming predictions too — "If H is true, we should NOT see W." A hypothesis you cannot state a disconfirmation for is not testable.
  5. Test each prediction against the evidence register and any additional data supplied. Record evidence for AND against — no hypothesis gets a verdict from supporting evidence alone.
  6. Assign verdicts: SUPPORTED / CONTRADICTED / INCONCLUSIVE. A verdict of SUPPORTED requires at least one surviving disconfirmation test.
  7. List research gaps: what single piece of missing data would most change the verdict? (This is the highest-value next research action.)

Prompt to give Copilot

You are running Step A3 (Hypothesis Testing) of a Minto analysis.

Situation: [PASTE]
Complication: [PASTE]
Evidence register: [PASTE]
Additional data: [PASTE / attach]

Do this:
1. Generate 3–5 MECE hypotheses for the root cause. Include the explanation
   stakeholders already believe.
2. For each: write confirming predictions ("if true, data shows X") AND
   disconfirming predictions ("if true, data should NOT show W").
3. Test every prediction against the data. Record evidence for AND against.
4. Verdicts: SUPPORTED (needs a survived disconfirmation test) /
   CONTRADICTED / INCONCLUSIVE.
5. Output a table: Hypothesis | Predictions | Evidence for | Evidence
   against | Verdict.
6. Name the strongest hypothesis and the single missing data item that
   would most change your confidence.

Your review checklist

  • ✓ The stakeholders' favourite explanation is in the table and was genuinely tested
  • ✓ Hypotheses are MECE — no two are the same cause reworded; nothing obvious is missing
  • ✓ Every SUPPORTED verdict survived a disconfirmation attempt (not just accumulated friendly evidence)
  • ✓ Evidence-against columns are populated — empty ones mean Copilot didn't look
  • ✓ The named research gap is worth the cost of gathering — or you accept the uncertainty explicitly

Reference example (SME banking)

"Competitor pricing" was the CFO's belief. Test: if price were the cause, existing customers would churn and price objections would rise. Data: existing churn stable, objections DOWN 30%. Verdict: CONTRADICTED. The surviving hypothesis — competitor messaging reframed the market — passed its disconfirmation test (existing customers, who haven't seen the messaging at renewal yet, are unaffected).

GATE: verdicts grounded in for-AND-against evidence; obvious answer tested

Next: A4 — Derive the Question and answer it
A4

Derive the Question and answer it

Frame the real problem as one question; answer it from the evidence

Input: hypothesis verdicts (A3) · Output: the Question (one sentence), the Answer (one sentence)

Copilot's process — follow in order

  1. Re-read Situation + Complication + strongest hypothesis together. The Question is what the reader would now ask, knowing all three.
  2. Draft the Question. Test it three ways: (a) it is ONE question, not several bundled; (b) it is answerable with what you know or can decide; (c) answering it tells you what to do — if you could answer it and still not know your next move, it is the wrong question.
  3. Check the Question level: it should sit ABOVE the surface debate. In the SME case the surface question was "should we cut price?" — the real question was "how do we compete when the market has been reframed on a dimension we don't win on?"
  4. Answer the Question in one sentence, drawn strictly from the surviving hypothesis. The Answer is a claim about what is true or what to do — not a restatement of findings.
  5. Trace check: Answer ← strongest hypothesis ← surviving evidence. If any link needs new facts not in the register, flag it.

Prompt to give Copilot

You are running Step A4 (Question & Answer) of a Minto analysis.

Situation: [PASTE]  Complication: [PASTE]
Strongest hypothesis + evidence: [PASTE from A3]

Do this:
1. Derive THE question the reader would ask knowing the above. One question
   only. It must be answerable, and answering it must determine action.
2. Check the altitude: is there a deeper question beneath the surface
   debate? Propose both if so, and recommend one.
3. Answer the question in ONE sentence, using only the surviving
   hypothesis and its evidence.
4. Show the trace: Answer ← hypothesis ← evidence. Flag any gap.

Output: Question, Answer, trace, any flags.

Your review checklist

  • ✓ One question — no "and" joining two decisions
  • ✓ Answering it determines action (the "so that we know what to do" test)
  • ✓ It's the real question, not the surface debate
  • ✓ The Answer follows from A3's verdicts — you can walk the trace aloud

GATE: one question, action-determining, answer traces to evidence

Next: A5 — Generate the recommendation
A5

Generate the recommendation

Turn the answer into an actionable, costed, testable recommendation

Input: Question + Answer (A4) · Output: recommendation with resources, timeline, alternatives, success metrics

Copilot's process — follow in order

  1. Generate 3–4 genuine response options given the Answer — including "do nothing" as an honest baseline with its cost, not a strawman.
  2. Evaluate each option against: strategic fit (does it play to what the evidence says we win on?), cost, time to impact, reversibility, and risk if the analysis is wrong.
  3. Select and draft the recommendation: what, why (one clause tying to the Answer), resources, timeline, owner.
  4. Attach success metrics: 3–4 measures, each with baseline, target, and measurement date. A metric without a date is a hope.
  5. Attach a decision gate: the condition under which you would stop or revisit ("if X hasn't moved by [date], revisit before further spend"). This is what makes the recommendation safe to approve.
  6. State the key assumption: the one thing that, if false, invalidates the recommendation.

Prompt to give Copilot

You are running Step A5 (Recommendation) of a Minto analysis.

Question: [PASTE]  Answer: [PASTE]
Constraints: budget [X], timeline [Y], strategic context [Z]

Do this:
1. Generate 3–4 genuine options including "do nothing" with its real cost.
2. Score each: strategic fit / cost / time to impact / reversibility /
   risk-if-wrong. Short table.
3. Recommend one. Format: what, why (tie to the Answer), resources,
   timeline, owner.
4. Success metrics: 3–4, each with baseline, target, and measurement DATE.
5. Decision gate: the stop/revisit condition and date.
6. Key assumption: the one thing that, if false, kills this.

Output: options table, recommendation, metrics, gate, assumption.

Your review checklist

  • ✓ Alternatives are real — you could defend choosing any of them
  • ✓ "Do nothing" has an honest cost attached
  • ✓ Every metric has baseline + target + date
  • ✓ The decision gate makes this approvable by a cautious CFO
  • ✓ The key assumption is genuinely load-bearing (test: if false, would you still act?)

GATE: evidence-based, costed, gated, key assumption named

A6

Hand off to Flow B

Package the analysis for the presentation flow

Handoff package — carry all of this into Flow B

B1

Choose the format

The reader's state of mind determines the document shape — decide before building anything

Input: recommendation (A5 or direct) + reader context · Output: chosen format + structural implications

Copilot's process — follow in order

  1. Establish the reader's state: do they know there is a problem? Are they aligned or conflicted? Committed or uncertain?
  2. Map to a format using the table below. The format decides the key-line pattern — building the wrong format costs the full rebuild.
  3. State the structural implications: what the key-lines will look like, what the ask slide is.
FormatReader's stateKey-line patternThe ask
RecommendationKnows the problem, needs a solutionDesirable / Feasible / Viable (or Why / How / What-it's-worth)Approve the action
Options paperConflicted between pathsCriteria → options → scorecard → recommended optionChoose (with a steer)
DiagnosticSurprised by an event, needs explanationWhat happened → why (evidence) → implicationsAccept the explanation, agree next step
Project updateAligned, wants progress checkStatus vs. plan → variances → decisions neededNote progress, unblock decisions
Strategic planCommitted, needs the roadmapWhere we play → how we win → what it takesEndorse the plan and resourcing

Next: B2 — Frame the audience
B2

Frame the audience

What the reader believes determines where the argument must start

Input: format (B1) + reader knowledge · Output: reader profile: beliefs, decision sought, skeptics, presence

Copilot's process — follow in order

  1. Profile the decision-maker: role, what they already know (this defines what the Situation may assume), what they currently believe about the cause (this defines what must be dismantled), what they care about most (this defines the lead argument).
  2. Map the room: who else attends, who is skeptical and of what, who influences the decision-maker.
  3. Confirm presence: live pitch (SCQA delivered verbally, slides exhibit-led) vs. standalone document (SCQA in full prose, self-sufficient).
  4. Write the belief gap: "Reader currently believes [X]; the argument must move them to [Y]." This gap is what the whole pyramid exists to close.

Prompt to give Copilot

Format: [PASTE from B1]   Recommendation: [PASTE]
What I know about the reader and room: [PASTE]

Do this:
1. Reader profile: role / knows / believes / cares-about-most.
2. Room map: attendees, skeptics + what each is skeptical of.
3. Presence: live or standalone, duration/length limit.
4. Belief gap: "Reader believes [X]; argument must move them to [Y]."

Output the four items. Flag anything about the reader I need to confirm.

Your review checklist

  • ✓ "Knows" list is accurate — anything wrongly assumed known will make the Situation fail its Nod Test
  • ✓ "Believes" captures the belief you must dismantle (e.g. "it's a pricing problem")
  • ✓ Every named skeptic has a stated skepticism — feeds B5 pressure-testing
  • ✓ The belief gap is honest about the distance to travel

GATE: belief gap explicit; skeptics named with their skepticisms

Next: B3 — Draft SCQA and Governing Thought
B3

Draft SCQA and Governing Thought

The hook that earns the reader's attention before you prove anything

Input: reader profile (B2) + Situation/Complication (A2 if available) · Output: SCQA + Governing Thought + tone order

Copilot's process — follow in order

  1. Draft the Situation FROM THE READER'S KNOWLEDGE (B2's "knows" list) — it may differ from the analysis-phase Situation. Only what this reader accepts belongs here.
  2. Draft the Complication so it creates tension against what this reader cares about most.
  3. Derive the Question the reader would now ask. It must be the question your recommendation answers — if it isn't, either the SCQA or the recommendation is wrong.
  4. Draft the Governing Thought — three drafts, converging:
    • Draft 1: say what you want to happen (usually a WHAT)
    • Draft 2: add what it produces (the SO-WHAT: revenue, retention, cost, strategic position)
    • Draft 3: compress to ≤25 words, active voice, verb-led
  5. Choose the tone order: S-C-A (standard, builds tension), A-S-C (direct — lead with the answer for impatient senior readers), C-S-A (alarm first — for urgency). Justify against the reader profile.
  6. Run the full Nod Test: for each element, write the reader's predicted internal response. All four must be nods.

Prompt to give Copilot

You are running Step B3 (SCQA + Governing Thought).

Reader profile: [PASTE from B2]
Analysis Situation/Complication (if available): [PASTE from A2]
Recommendation: [PASTE]

Do this:
1. Draft Situation from what THIS reader already accepts (their "knows"
   list). 2–3 sentences, no controversy.
2. Draft Complication creating tension against what they care about most.
3. Derive the Question. Verify: does my recommendation answer exactly this
   question?
4. Governing Thought in 3 converging drafts (WHAT → +SO-WHAT → ≤25 words,
   active, verb-led). Show all three drafts.
5. Recommend tone order (S-C-A / A-S-C / C-S-A) for this reader. Justify.
6. Nod Test: for each element, write the reader's predicted internal
   response. Flag any non-nod and propose the repair.

Your review checklist — the Nod Test in full

  • ✓ Situation → reader thinks "yes, that's where we are" (not "who says?")
  • ✓ Complication → reader thinks "yes, that's a real problem" (not "is it though?")
  • ✓ Question → reader thinks "yes, that's exactly what I'm now wondering"
  • ✓ Answer → reader thinks "if that's true, it resolves my question" (belief comes later — the pyramid's job)
  • ✓ Governing Thought: ≤25 words, starts with a verb, states what it PRODUCES, falsifiable
  • ✓ Tone order fits the reader (senior/impatient → consider A-S-C)

Governing Thought: three-draft example (FFM)

Draft 1 (WHAT): "We should build an AI food assistant for families."

Draft 2 (better, wordy): "We should launch Fairy Food Mother, an AI-powered food assistant that drives habit over discount dependency, converts 20% of Segment B, and generates £3.9M..."

Draft 3 (≤25 words, SO-WHAT): "Launch Fairy Food Mother: a family food assistant converting young families from discount-dependent to habit-driven, generating £3.9M annual revenue at 60%+ retention."

GATE: four nods; Governing Thought ≤25 words, verb-led, SO-WHAT

Next: B4 — Build the pyramid
B4

Build the pyramid

Key-lines and sub-points, audited against vertical logic, horizontal logic, MECE and order

Input: Governing Thought (B3) + evidence register (A2/A3) · Output: audited pyramid: 3–5 key-lines, each with 3–5 sub-points

Copilot's process — follow in order

  1. Ask of the Governing Thought: "Why is this true?" (or for an action: "Why should we do this?"). The answers are candidate key-lines. Use the format's pattern from B1 (e.g. Desirable/Feasible/Viable).
  2. Decide the grouping logic and keep it consistent:
    • Inductive (usual for recommendations): parallel independent reasons, each sufficient to matter alone. Test each pair: "are these the same KIND of idea?"
    • Deductive (for logical chains): premise → premise → therefore. Test: does the conclusion actually require both premises? Never mix deductive and inductive in one grouping.
  3. Run MECE on the key-line set:
    • Mutually exclusive: no two key-lines are the same argument reworded ("improve retention" + "reduce churn" = one argument twice). Test: could one exhibit prove both? Then merge.
    • Collectively exhaustive: nothing the reader needs is missing. Test: "if all key-lines are true, MUST the reader accept the Governing Thought?" If they could accept all three and still say no, something is missing.
  4. Choose ONE order and state it: time (first to last), structure (by part), or degree (most important first). Recommendations usually take degree order.
  5. Under each key-line, place 3–5 sub-points from the evidence register. Test vertical logic at every joint: the sub-points must ANSWER the question the key-line raises ("why?"/"how?"), and the key-line must genuinely summarise them — not merely introduce them.
  6. Kill orphans: any sub-point that doesn't support its key-line moves or dies. Any assertion with no evidence beneath it gets flagged for B6 (exhibit needed) or cut.
  7. Output the audit explicitly: MECE result, order chosen, logic type per grouping, vertical-logic check per key-line.

Prompt to give Copilot

You are running Step B4 (Pyramid Build).

Governing Thought: [PASTE]
Format key-line pattern: [PASTE from B1]
Evidence register: [PASTE from A2/A3]

Do this:
1. Generate 3–5 key-lines answering "why is the Governing Thought true?"
   following the format pattern.
2. Declare grouping logic (inductive or deductive) and keep it consistent.
3. MECE audit: (a) any two key-lines provable by the same exhibit? merge.
   (b) If all key-lines are true, MUST the reader accept the Governing
   Thought? If not, what's missing?
4. Declare ONE order (time / structure / degree) and apply it.
5. Place 3–5 sub-points per key-line from the evidence register only.
6. Vertical logic check per key-line: do the sub-points answer the "why?"
   the key-line raises? Does the key-line SUMMARISE (not introduce) them?
7. Flag orphan sub-points and unevidenced assertions.

Output: the pyramid + explicit audit results for 3, 4, 6, 7.

Your review checklist

  • ✓ Vertical logic: read each key-line, ask "why?", read its sub-points — do they answer it completely?
  • ✓ Summary test: each key-line could be reconstructed from its sub-points alone
  • ✓ MECE: no overlap (one-exhibit test) and no gap (if-all-true-must-accept test)
  • ✓ One order, stated, and it serves the reader (degree order for busy executives)
  • ✓ Same kind: all key-lines are arguments (not a mix of argument + evidence + plan)
  • ✓ No orphan sub-points; every assertion has evidence beneath it or a flag for B6

Vertical logic worked example

Key-line: "Churn is perception-driven, not price-driven." Reader asks: "why do you say that?" Sub-points must answer exactly that: (a) existing customers stable — price pain would hit them first; (b) price objections down 30%; (c) all 10 lost deals cite the competitor by name; (d) deal sizes unchanged — closers accept current pricing. Each sub-point answers the "why". That is vertical logic holding.

GATE: vertical logic holds at every joint; MECE passes both tests; one order

Next: B5 — Pressure-test the argument
B5

Pressure-test the argument

Attack every key-line with the room's actual skeptics before they do

Input: pyramid (B4) + skeptic map (B2) + contradicted hypotheses (A3) · Output: objection matrix, repairs applied, revised ask

Copilot's process — follow in order

  1. For each key-line, generate the strongest objection — voiced as the NAMED skeptic from B2 would voice it, not a generic devil's advocate.
  2. Draft the counter for each. A counter must cite evidence from the register; a counter that is just louder assertion fails.
  3. Classify each counter: HOLDS (evidence exists), NEEDS EVIDENCE (obtainable — add to B6 list), CONCEDE & REFRAME (objection is right — revise the pyramid now, don't defend the indefensible).
  4. Identify the least-evidenced argument in the whole pyramid. Decide: strengthen it, or shrink its role (a weak moat argument can become "table stakes" rather than a pillar).
  5. Use A3's contradicted hypotheses offensively: the reader's likely belief was tested and failed — plan where that lands (usually early, to clear the ground).
  6. Revise the ask if pressure-testing exposed risk: add the decision gate to the ask itself ("approve X; gate at 90 days on metric Y") — a gated ask is easier to approve.

Prompt to give Copilot

You are running Step B5 (Pressure-Test).

Pyramid: [PASTE from B4]
Skeptics + their skepticisms: [PASTE from B2]
Contradicted hypotheses + evidence: [PASTE from A3]

Do this:
1. For each key-line: strongest objection AS THE NAMED SKEPTIC would voice
   it. No generic objections.
2. Counter each, citing evidence from the register. No assertion-only
   counters.
3. Classify each counter: HOLDS / NEEDS EVIDENCE (add to exhibit list) /
   CONCEDE & REFRAME (propose the pyramid revision).
4. Name the least-evidenced argument. Recommend: strengthen or shrink.
5. Plan where the contradicted stakeholder belief gets dismantled in the
   flow (usually early).
6. Revise the ask to include the decision gate.

Output: objection matrix, revisions to pyramid, revised ask.

Your review checklist

  • ✓ Objections sound like your actual room, not a textbook
  • ✓ Every HOLDS counter cites evidence you could produce live
  • ✓ CONCEDE items actually changed the pyramid (check B4 output was revised)
  • ✓ The weakest argument is now either strengthened or demoted — not left load-bearing
  • ✓ The ask now contains its own decision gate

GATE: every counter evidenced or conceded; ask is gated

Next: B6 — Design the exhibits
B6

Design the exhibits

Message first, chart second — the discipline that separates consulting-grade exhibits from decorated data

Input: revised pyramid (B5) + evidence register · Output: exhibit specification sheet, one spec per exhibit

The iron rule: the message determines the chart

Never start from the data ("what chart fits these numbers?"). Start from the assertion ("what must this exhibit prove?"), then pick the one chart form that shows exactly that comparison. If you cannot write the assertion title first, you are not ready to build the exhibit.

Copilot's process — follow in order, per exhibit

  1. Write the assertion title first. Take the sub-point. Write the title as a full-sentence claim with the "so what" included. This title is fixed BEFORE any chart choice.
    • Label: "Q4 lost-deal analysis"
    • Assertion: "All 10 Q4 lost deals cite the competitor — none cite price"
  2. Classify the comparison. Every quantitative message is one of five comparison types. The type dictates the chart family:
    Comparison typeMessage keywordsChart form
    Component (share of a whole)"share of", "percentage of", "X% of total"Pie (≤5 slices) or 100% stacked bar
    Item (ranking things)"larger than", "ranked", "more/less than"Horizontal bar
    Time series (change over time)"grew", "declined", "since", "trend"Column (few periods) or line (many)
    Frequency (distribution)"concentration", "range", "most fall between"Histogram / column
    Correlation (relationship)"relates to", "increases with", "depends on"Scatter or paired horizontal bar
  3. One message per exhibit. If the assertion contains "and" joining two claims, split into two exhibits. A chart proving two things proves neither memorably.
  4. Choose the evidence type where the claim is not quantitative: quote strip (verbatim customer/analyst quotes with attribution), comparison table (us vs. them on named dimensions), mockup (for product propositions), process/flywheel diagram (for mechanisms), scenario table (for financial cases — always pessimistic/central/optimistic, never a single point estimate).
  5. Specify the highlight. Every exhibit has one visual emphasis matching the title's claim: the one bar coloured differently, the divergence circled, the row bolded. If everything is emphasised, nothing is.
  6. Strip the decoration: no 3D, no background fills, no legend where direct labels fit, no gridline clutter, sensible rounding (£3.9M, not £3,912,447), axis starting at zero unless the deviation IS the message (then annotate the break).
  7. Add source and units to every exhibit: "Source: CRM lost-deal records, Oct–Dec 2025. n=10." An unsourced exhibit is an anecdote.
  8. Write the spec sheet row for each exhibit: ID, sub-point served, assertion title, comparison type, chart form, data needed, highlight, source, status (data ready / to gather).

Next: B7 — Write the narrative
B7

Write the narrative

SCQA introduction, body by key-line, Minto conclusion — under full copy standards

Input: pyramid (B5) + exhibit specs (B6) + SCQA (B3) · Output: complete narrative + speaker notes

Copilot's process — follow in order

  1. Write the introduction as the SCQA in the chosen tone order. In prose, the introduction must be readable in under 30 seconds and end with the Governing Thought.
  2. Preview the key-lines in one sentence immediately after the Governing Thought ("Three things make this the right move: it is desirable, deliverable and defensible.").
  3. Write the body: one section per key-line, in the pyramid's order. Section pattern: heading = the key-line assertion → why it matters (2–3 sentences) → the evidence, referencing exhibits by their assertion titles → transition planting the next key-line's question.
  4. Write the conclusion in Minto's three-part structure — no other structure is acceptable:
    • Restate the Governing Thought (fresh words, same claim)
    • Name the key assumption: "This rests on [assumption]; if [signal] we revisit at [gate]" — naming it disarms the skeptic who was waiting to raise it
    • Next action: who does what, starting when
  5. Apply the copy standards below to every sentence — mechanically, not impressionistically.
  6. Split speaker notes from narrative: details, statistics and story colour that support delivery but would clutter the page.

Copy standards — apply mechanically (Minto Part 12)

  1. Active voice. Subject acts. "We will reposition" not "repositioning will be undertaken".
  2. Kill nominalisations. Words ending -tion/-ment/-ance hiding a verb: "utilisation of" → "use"; "implementation of X requires" → "to implement X, we must".
  3. ≤25 words per sentence. Count. Break at subordinate clauses.
  4. One idea per sentence. An "and" joining two claims = two sentences.
  5. Numbers: spell one–nine; numerals 10+; dates as 8 May 2026; every % has a referent ("35% of SME accounts", never bare "35%").
  6. Punctuation: no exclamation marks; no Oxford comma; em dashes spaced and rare.
  7. Capitals: proper nouns only. "the chief financial officer", "the repositioning campaign".
  8. No hedging without content. "It could be argued that" = delete. Either make the argument or cut it.

B8

Run the lint check

Sixteen mechanical rules + six diagnostics — the pre-flight inspection before anything is built or sent

Input: everything produced so far · Output: lint report — every rule PASS or repaired

The 16 lint rules

  1. Governing Thought ≤25 words, active voice, verb-led
  2. Governing Thought states a SO-WHAT (what it produces), not a WHAT
  3. SCQA contains exactly one Question
  4. Situation contains no contested claim and no "but/however"
  5. Every heading and slide title is a full-sentence assertion — testable, falsifiable
  6. 3–5 key-lines, never more
  7. Key-lines are MECE (one-exhibit overlap test; if-all-true-must-accept gap test)
  8. Every key-line summarises its sub-points (reconstructable-from-below test)
  9. Each grouping is purely inductive or purely deductive — never mixed
  10. Each grouping follows ONE declared order: time, structure or degree
  11. No orphan assertions — every claim has evidence beneath it
  12. Every exhibit title is an assertion, not a label
  13. One message per exhibit; every exhibit has source + units
  14. Sentences ≤25 words, active voice, no nominalisations
  15. Numbers formatted: one–nine spelled, 10+ numerals, dates as 8 May 2026, every % has a referent
  16. Conclusion restates Governing Thought + names key assumption + next action

The 6-check final diagnostic

  1. Title-read test: read ONLY the headings/slide titles in sequence. Does the full argument hold with nothing else?
  2. Nod test: SCQA still earns four nods from the named reader?
  3. So-what test: for every section/slide ask "so what?" — anything without an answer gets cut
  4. Skeptic pass: every named skeptic's objection has an evidenced counter placed in the flow
  5. Evidence trace: every number traces to the register; every register source could be produced live
  6. Single ask: the document asks for exactly one decision, gated, with owner and date

Storyboard the slides

Dot-dash the titles first, then hang exhibits on them — never the other way round

Input: linted narrative (B8) + exhibit specs (B6) · Output: slide-by-slide storyboard with anatomy per slide

Copilot's process — follow in order

  1. Dot-dash the deck first. Before any slide is designed, write the title sequence alone — one assertion per line, in order. Read it aloud. This IS the presentation; slides merely evidence it. If the sequence has a gap or a jump, fix it here, at zero cost.
  2. Standard sequence for a Recommendation deck: cover → Governing Thought → context (situation) → complication → key-line 1 (1 intro + 1–3 exhibit slides) → transition → key-line 2 (same) → key-line 3 (same) → conclusion/assumption → the gated ask. Target: 20-minute pitch ≈ 12–16 slides.
  3. Build each slide to the standard anatomy:
    • Title zone: the assertion, ≤2 lines. The only element that MUST be read.
    • Exhibit zone: ~90% of the body — the exhibit from its B6 spec, with its single highlight.
    • Source line: bottom, small: source, date, n=, units.
    TITLE = full-sentence assertion (≤2 lines) — the only must-read element
    optional kicker: one orienting clause
    EXHIBIT — fills ~90% of body
    one message · one highlight · direct labels
    Source: [system], [date range], n=[..] · units: [..]
  4. Enforce per-slide discipline: one slide = one assertion = one exhibit. A slide needing two exhibits is two slides. A slide whose title contains "and" joining claims is two slides.
  5. Push all explanation to speaker notes. Bullet text on the slide competes with the exhibit and loses.
  6. Design the two text-only slides deliberately: the Governing Thought slide (large type, clean background, nothing else) and the ask slide (the decision, the gate, the date — nothing else).
  7. Re-run the title-read test on the final storyboard: titles alone, in order, must carry the complete argument.
